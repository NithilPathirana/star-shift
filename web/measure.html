Measure it yourself

How fast does Barnard's Star move across the sky? Find out with two real NASA SPHEREx pictures, a ruler made of pixels and a little maths. A classroom activity for ages 11 and up, about 10 minutes.

Loading the saved SPHEREx images of Barnard's Star…

1Blink the first and last seasons

SPHEREx photographs the same patch of sky about every six months. Each picture here is many images from one visit (a season) stacked together. Press Blink and watch the bright star in the middle: it hops a tiny bit. That hop is Barnard's Star moving through space.

2Click the star's centre in each picture

The same two pictures, zoomed in on the middle. Click the very centre of the star's glow in each one. A cross marks your click, and a dashed ring shows your click from the other picture, so you can see the hop. Keyboard: focus a picture, then use the arrow keys to move the cross (hold Shift to move a whole pixel).

The dotted rings mark where Gaia's catalogue says the star is in each season. Try to find its centre yourself first!

3How far did it move?

Each pixel in these pictures covers 6.15″ (arcseconds) of sky. An arcsecond is tiny: 1″ is about the width of a small coin seen from 4 km away.

Click the star in both pictures first.

4Divide by the time between the pictures

Speed = distance ÷ time. For each picture we use the average date of the images stacked in it.

Waiting for step 3.

5Compare with our fit and with Gaia

Star Shift's computer found the star in all 120 images and fitted a straight line through its positions (see the Proof page). The Gaia spacecraft measured it far more precisely. How close did you get?

Waiting for your speed.
